## Post generado

> Causal ordering is one of those concurrency properties that stays invisible — right up until it isn't.
> 
> A newly added corpus entry flags a sharp finding: Remix's concurrent form submissions have a structural flaw. Without causal ordering, concurrent submissions can race, interleave database reads and writes, and produce state that no user ever intended. The write-up makes this concrete with timelines, not just theory.
> 
> What makes this worth curating alongside a Python free-threading piece and a Quarkus benchmark entry isn't that it's a gotcha against one framework. It's that the failure mode generalizes. Optimistic concurrency, eventual UI consistency, "fire and handle" submission patterns — they all share the same quiet assumption: that operations arriving close together in time are safe to process independently.
> 
> Free-threaded Python (the same batch, different row) is lifting the GIL to unlock real parallelism. Quarkus benchmarks are measuring startup and throughput tradeoffs at the platform level. Both are expansions of what "concurrent" means in production.
> 
> The Remix entry is the counterweight. Concurrency you didn't ask for — race conditions in a UI submission flow — is just as real as concurrency you engineered deliberately. And the fix isn't "serialize everything." It's causal ordering: knowing which operations depend on which prior state and respecting that chain.
> 
> The corpus now holds both sides: the performance wins from parallelism, and the correctness cost of ignoring ordering guarantees.
> 
> That's a more honest picture of what concurrent systems actually require.

The remaining nodes were considered to have unique upgrade domains since their xfer-address was considered as the upgrade domain.
